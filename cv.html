Meta's product catalog ↗ powers the product information shown across ads and commerce experiences on Facebook and Instagram.

Sophisticated advertisers often needed more control over how their products were represented to buyers. Some needed greater control over how product information was prioritised across multiple data sources. Others needed products to appear differently across objectives, but their only option was maintaining separate catalogs.

Through research and strategy work, we recognised that seemingly different advertiser requests were manifestations of the same underlying need: greater control over how products appeared to buyers. Solving those needs independently risked creating disconnected experiences as new forms of product control were introduced. That insight shaped two phases of work: Configure Sources, which established transparency and control over product data, and Product Data Versions, which extended that control across objectives.

Design principles

With two phases addressing the same underlying goal from different angles, I created design principles to keep decisions coherent across both.

Transparency before control

Advertisers needed to understand their data before they could confidently change it. Configure Sources surfaces which source is winning for each attribute before offering controls to change it. Product Data Versions lets advertisers validate that version data is correct before anything goes live.

Build on what advertisers already know

Advertisers shouldn't need to learn a different workflow for different types of catalog control. Configure Sources and Product Data Versions share the same prioritisation patterns and concepts, so each new capability builds on what advertisers already know, rather than introducing new mental models from scratch.

Build foundations that can evolve

Localisation wasn't part of the beta, but future needs like it had to be considered from the start. Making those decisions early meant new capabilities could be added without rethinking how product representation worked.

Configure Sources

Configure Sources addressed a long-standing advertiser request: greater control over how conflicting product information was resolved.

We knew advertisers needed transparency and control. The question was how those controls should work. Across research and iteration, we repeatedly saw that advertisers needed to understand why the system was behaving the way it was before they felt confident changing it. That principle shaped the final experience.

Surfacing conflicts before offering controls

Advertisers couldn't fix what they couldn't see. Attributes where sources disagree are surfaced first, ordered by how consequential a conflict is. From there, advertisers drag to reprioritise, changing which source wins for any attribute.

Making the default visible

Meta had always chosen a winning source if there was a conflict, advertisers just couldn't see which one, or why. Now, the design surfaces that choice explicitly, so advertisers can make an informed decision about their data. They can override it, but at least one source must always be set, so buyers always see accurate product information.

Results

By making prioritisation transparent before making it configurable, the catalog stopped being something that happened to advertisers and became something they could actively control.

Outcomes

  • Exceeded the revenue protection goal by 2x, protecting hundreds of millions in advertiser revenue
  • Unblocked advertisers who couldn't run product ads without source-level control
  • Saved 2.66 engineering years previously spent manually resolving data conflicts

What it unlocked

  • Established the foundation for Product Data Versions and future objective-specific catalog customisation within a single catalog

Product Data Versions

Configure Sources established transparency and control over product data. Product Data Versions built on that foundation, giving advertisers more flexibility in how their products appeared depending on where and how they were advertising.

Advertisers had long needed this flexibility, but the existing catalog wasn't designed for it. The challenge wasn't just introducing a new capability. We needed a clear definition of what versions should do—one grounded in how advertisers actually wanted to use them and flexible enough to support future needs as the product evolved.

Defining what a version is

Advertisers previously had to maintain separate catalogs to show products differently across use cases, a workaround that hurt performance. Product Data Versions solved that by letting advertisers create alternate representations of their products without changing what buyers see by default.

One of the most important decisions was defining what versions should actually do. Advertisers weren't looking for a way to create new products. They wanted more control over how existing products were presented depending on where and how they were running ads.

Getting that definition right helped create a product that was more coherent, easier to position, and flexible enough to support future use cases without reinventing the model each time.

Validate products before running ads

Product Data Versions introduced more flexibility, but also more complexity. Advertisers needed confidence that the right products and information would appear before spending money on ads.

Validation tools let advertisers confirm everything looks right before anything runs: that data applied correctly, that the right products are included, and that ads will appear as intended.

Building confidence into the workflow was as important as the capability itself.

Results

Outcomes

  • Satisfaction scores averaged 8–9/10 across exit interviews
  • Consistent ROAS improvement1 reported across the beta cohort

What it unlocked

Key Takeaway

The most important work on this project wasn't designing screens. It was helping define what product representation should be before those decisions became embedded in the system.

The advertiser research, design vision, and foundational behaviour work all served the same goal: creating a product grounded in how advertisers actually worked, while giving the business room to evolve it over time.

Design's job isn't just to make things look right. It's to make sure the thinking behind the product is as considered as the product itself.
